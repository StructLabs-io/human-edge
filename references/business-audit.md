# Human-edge audit: where AI should take over, and where a person has to show up

For a coach working with a business owner, or an owner auditing their own business. The audit walks through the business one customer touchpoint at a time and sorts each one into four buckets. The result is a one-page Human Edge Map and one change to make this month.

The idea behind it: most business looks like transactions, but behind the transactions are people. In one B2B study, customers' loyalty to the *person* they dealt with drove sales growth more than loyalty to the firm ([Palmatier et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.44.2.185)), and that people value a human who chose to show up, especially when trust or emotion is involved ([Rubin et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02247-w)). So automate the transactions to buy time, and spend that time where a person matters most. The evidence behind each check below is in `research/business-and-coaching.md`.

## The four buckets

| Bucket | Who does it | Fits when | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Automate** | AI does it; a person spot-checks | Routine, low stakes, rule-based; the customer cares about speed and accuracy, not who did it | Booking confirmations, invoice reminders, FAQ answers, data entry, first-draft summaries for internal use |
| **Assist** | AI drafts; a person edits, decides and signs | AI is good at the task, but the output carries the business's name | Proposals, marketing copy, reports, routine client updates |
| **Own** | A person decides; AI gives an independent view | Judgment calls with real consequences | Pricing exceptions, hiring, strategy, disputes, credit decisions |
| **Show up** | A person, live: face to face or on a call | Trust, emotion, conflict, big decisions, or the relationship itself is the product | First meeting, bad news, complaints, renewals, key accounts, apologies, negotiations |

## How to run it

Go one step at a time and ask one question at a time. For each touchpoint, have the owner note their own sort first, on paper or in a note, not in this chat. Then give yours, and only then compare. If you see their call before you make yours, you'll tend to agree with it, and they lose the second opinion. It's their business, and the audit should sharpen their judgment, not replace it.

**If a coach is preparing to run this with a client:** do it twice. In prep, the coach sorts the touchpoints first and you challenge them; that becomes the coach's private draft. In the session, the owner sorts first, and the coach keeps the draft face-down until the owner has made their own calls. Finish prep with a short run sheet for the session.

### Step 1: Context (2–3 questions)
What does the business sell, to whom, and where does most of the revenue come from: new customers, repeat customers, or referrals? Who in the business holds the key relationships?

### Step 2: Map the journey
List 8 to 15 touchpoints from first contact to repeat purchase or referral. Add the internal decisions that shape the business: pricing, hiring, which customers to take on. Write them in the order the customer experiences them.

### Step 3: Sort each touchpoint
For each one, ask four questions:
1. **Stakes:** if this goes wrong, what does it cost in money, trust or legal exposure?
2. **Trust and emotion:** is the customer anxious, upset, about to make a big decision, or deciding whether to trust you?
3. **Who do they think they're dealing with?** Would they feel let down if they found out AI did this?
4. **Is AI actually good at it, and can someone check its output?** AI does well on tasks inside its abilities and markedly worse on tasks just outside them (the "jagged frontier"; [Dell'Acqua et al., 2026; working paper 2023](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838)). Test before trusting.

Low stakes, low emotion, AI is good → **Automate**. AI is good but it carries your name → **Assist**. Consequential judgment → **Own**. Trust, emotion or relationship at the centre → **Show up**.

### Step 4: Check the risks
- **Hidden AI.** If a customer would feel deceived on finding out AI handled something, either say so openly ("drafted with AI, checked by me") or move it up a bucket. In experiments, disclosing AI use cost some trust, but being found out by someone else cost more ([Schilke & Reimann, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405)).
- **A path to a human.** Every automated touchpoint needs a visible way to reach a person.
  - Customers are more willing to use AI when there's a clear route to a human ([Salesforce, 2024](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/ai-customer-research/)).
  - Losing access to a person is their top fear about AI service, and 53% said they'd consider switching to a competitor ([Gartner, 2024](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-09-gartner-survey-finds-64-percent-of-customers-would-prefer-that-companies-didnt-use-ai-for-customer-service)).
  - Gartner also predicts that by 2027 half of organisations will abandon plans to cut service staff because of AI ([2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-10-gartner-predicts-50-percent-of-organizations-will-abandon-plans-to-reduce-customer-service-workforce-due-to-ai)).
- **The business answers for its AI.** A tribunal held Air Canada responsible for what its chatbot told a customer ([Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024](https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/item/525448/index.do)), and courts have logged over 2,000 cases of people filing AI-invented content ([Charlotin database](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/)). Anything AI sends a customer in the business's name needs a named person who checks it, or a narrow, tested scope.
- **Key-person risk.** If customer loyalty sits with one person, it can walk out with them.
  - In a study of B2B sales, loyalty to the salesperson, not the firm, drove sales growth, which is exactly what leaves with them ([Palmatier, Scheer & Steenkamp, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.44.2.185)).
  - Spread key relationships: a second contact, and shared notes on what each client cares about.
- **Who's stronger at the decision?** On average, human–AI teams did worse than the better of the two alone, with losses concentrated in decision tasks, and teams lost ground when the AI alone was stronger than the human ([Vaccaro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1)). For each "Own" decision, be honest about whether the person or the AI is better at it, and design around that.
- **Juniors who never learn.** If junior staff use AI for the core of their work, they may never build the skill: learning studies show roughly a fifth lower performance once AI is removed ([Bastani et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [Shen & Tamkin, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245)). AI also lifts novices most (+34% for new support agents; [Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)), so the gain is real while the learning risk is hidden. Keep some training tasks human-first.
- **Local norms.** In Malaysia and much of Asia, face and relationships carry weight. In our experience (not a research finding), bad news, negotiations, collections and apologies usually belong in **Show up**, even when AI could write the words.
- **Don't overestimate the human premium here.** People in emerging economies trust AI more than people in advanced ones ([KPMG and University of Melbourne, 2025](https://kpmg.com/za/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025/05/global-study-reveals-trust-of-ai-remains-a-critical-challenge-reflecting-tension-between-benefits-and-risks.html); Malaysia wasn't in that survey). In Malaysia, 37% say they trust AI tools enough not to check their work, against 31% globally and 23% in the US ([Ipsos AI Monitor, 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-06/AI%20Monitor%202026%20%28English%29.pdf)). Customers may accept more automation than US surveys suggest. Ask your own customers.

### Step 5: Where do the freed hours go?
Automation is only half the point. Ask: "If AI takes these tasks, how many hours a week come back, and which relationships or moments get them?" Name three specific customers, partners or team members who get more of a person's time.

### If the owner wants to cut headcount
Keep two questions apart: *where AI goes* (this audit) and *who goes* (a people decision). Settle the first before the second.
- **Pilot before you cut.** Run the change for 30 days on one workflow and measure what actually freed up. Measure, don't estimate: in one small study with early-2025 tools, experienced developers believed AI had sped them up by 20% when it had slowed them by 19%, though the same lab's 2026 update points the other way ([METR, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Quality problems show up with customers first; Klarna brought humans back to support after leaning hard on AI ([Fortune, 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/05/09/klarna-ai-humans-return-on-investment/)).
- **Check what customers are actually buying.** If clients are pushing prices down, the problem may be pricing power, not headcount. Cutting people can cost more in lost relationships than it saves.
- **Treat the team honestly.** People find out. How the owner talks about AI with staff shapes who stays.
- **Get local employment and legal advice** before any retrenchment; the rules on notice, benefits and process vary by country and change.

### Step 6: One change this month
Pick one thing to automate and one moment to show up more. Give each an owner and a date. Small and done beats big and planned.

## Output: the Human Edge Map

Give the owner a clean block like this to keep:

```

| # | Touchpoint | Today | Recommended | Why | Risk check |

| 1 | ...        | ...   | Automate / Assist / Own / Show up | ... | path to human / disclosure / key person |

- Automate: ______ (owner, date)

```

## Coaching notes

- **Push back on both extremes.** "Automate everything" ignores that customers buy from people. "AI can't do what we do" usually isn't true for the written parts of the work: AI already matches people on written empathy and persuasion (`research/ai-already-matches.md`), and customer-service and sales tasks rank among the most AI-applicable work ([Tomlinson et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07935)). The edge is in who shows up and who owns the call.
- **Use the evidence lightly.** One sentence of research, at the moment it helps the decision, beats a lecture.
- **Coaching businesses.** If the business *is* coaching or advice:
  - In one trial, human coaching beat a scripted AI chatbot on goals and stress ([de Haan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13678868.2026.2633990)).
  - BetterUp says half of its users want both a human coach and AI, 34% only a human and 15% only AI (company figures with no method given; [BetterUp, 2025](https://www.betterup.com/press/betterup-launches-ai-coaching)).
  - Design for "both": AI between sessions, the coach in the room.
- **Keep data safe.** Use roles or initials, not client names, if this conversation runs in an AI tool.
- **Short example** (illustrative only): a renovation contractor automates appointment reminders and progress photos (Automate), drafts quotes with AI but signs them personally (Assist), keeps variation pricing (Own), and makes sure the owner personally calls every client when something goes wrong on site (Show up). Freed hours go into a walkthrough with each client at handover, which is where referrals come from.
